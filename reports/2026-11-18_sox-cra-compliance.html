SOX and the Cyber Resilience Act

One law audits the financial reporting of US-listed companies; the other gates EU market access for anything with digital elements. What each regime actually requires as of 18 November 2026, where their controls overlap, and the software — open source and commercial — that carries the compliance load.

00The answer, in one page

Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) and the Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) solve different problems with different machinery. SOX protects the truthfulness of financial reporting: it binds issuers listed on US markets, makes CEOs and CFOs certify controls personally, and backs failure with prison time (§802: up to 20 years). The CRA protects the cybersecurity of products: it binds anyone who makes software or hardware "with digital elements" available on the EU market — US companies included — and backs failure with fines up to €15 million or 2.5% of worldwide turnover, plus withdrawal of CE marking, plus — uniquely — a 24-hour regulator notification clock that has already been running since 11 September 2026.

The regimes do not conflict; they interlock. A US-listed software vendor selling into the EU answers to both, and roughly two-thirds of the underlying control work — change management, access management, vulnerability handling, monitoring, evidence retention — is the same control fabric serving two reporting formats. Build the fabric once; map it twice.

The bottom line The urgent one is the CRA, and the most expensive misunderstanding in the market is "we have until December 2027". Reporting duties (Art 14) have applied since 11 September 2026, to all products made available on the EU market including ones placed there before the full application date — so a shipped product with an actively exploited vulnerability starts a 24-hour early-warning clock today. Meanwhile the CRA's harmonised-standards machinery is behind schedule (the standardisation request was extended by three months on 10 November 2026), which means early movers self-assess against Annex I directly while everyone else waits for standards that will arrive late.

For software buyers: SOX tooling is a mature, consolidated market (ServiceNow, Workiva, AuditBoard, SAP GRC at the top; Drata/Vanta compressing the mid-market), and the open source side has quietly become capable enough to run the whole ICFR evidence pipeline (Wazuh, ComplianceAsCode, InSpec, Gitea/GitLab CE, Teleport). CRA tooling is younger: the SBOM/SCA segment (Syft, Trivy, OSV-Scanner — OSS; Snyk, Black Duck, FOSSA — commercial) covers the technical spine, but nobody fully productises "Annex I conformity" yet, because the standards themselves are still being written.

01The two regimes at a glance

Same century, different theory of regulation. SOX is a disclosure-and-attestation law: government sets the duty to certify, the market (auditors, investors) polices the content. The CRA is a product-safety law grafted onto the New Legislative Framework: government sets essential requirements, CE marking signals conformity, market surveillance authorities can pull products off the Union market.

Three structural differences that matter (1) SOX bites individuals (officers sign, officers go to prison); the CRA bites products (CE marking can be withdrawn, products recalled). (2) SOX is scoped by who you are (an issuer); the CRA by what you sell where (a connected product in the EU). (3) SOX runs on an annual audit rhythm; the CRA runs on an event rhythm — the 24-hour clock starts when you become aware, not when the fiscal year ends.

02SOX: what the law actually says

Passed after Enron and WorldCom, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (Pub. L. 107-204, 30 July 2002) rewired US capital markets: it created the PCAOB to inspect auditors, made officers personally certify reports, and criminalised record destruction. Two decades later it is the most rehearsed compliance exercise in software companies' calendars. The sections that shape daily engineering practice:

Section Codified What it requires (verified against the US Code)
§302 15 U.S.C. §7241 CEO/CFO certify in every periodic report: they reviewed it; no material misstatements; internal controls established, designed, evaluated within 90 days; all significant deficiencies, material weaknesses and fraud disclosed to auditors and the audit committee; subsequent changes reported.
§404(a) 15 U.S.C. §7262(a) Annual internal control report: management states responsibility for ICFR and assesses its effectiveness as of fiscal year end. Applies to all reporting issuers.
§404(b) 15 U.S.C. §7262(b)-(c) The auditor attests to management's assessment. Exempt: emerging growth companies, and issuers that are neither large accelerated nor accelerated filers (i.e. float under $75M or SRC-eligible).
§409 15 U.S.C. §78m(l) Real-time disclosure: material changes in financial condition or operations on Form 8-K "rapidly and current"; material cybersecurity incidents have had their own line item — Item 1.05, Cybersecurity Incidents — since December 2023, with the default four-business-day 8-K clock.

§906 18 U.S.C. §1350 Criminal certification liability: knowing violation up to $1M / 10 years; willful up to $5M / 20 years.

The machine under the certifications: ICFR and ITGC

Everything funnels into internal control over financial reporting. The CFR defines the two failure states auditors escalate: a significant deficiency ("less severe than a material weakness yet important enough to merit attention by those responsible for oversight") and a material weakness ("a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement … will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis"). A material weakness means an adverse ICFR opinion, an amended §404 disclosure, and a very bad quarter.

Because books live in software, auditors test IT general controls — the four classic domains, unchanged in practice for two decades:

ITGC ├── Access to programs and data who has privileged access, provisioning, │ deprovisioning, periodic user access reviews ├── Program changes change control: ticket → approval → test → │ segregation of duties → production deploy ├── Program development SDLC discipline for new systems feeding the │ financial statements └── Computer operations batch jobs, backups, incident handling, interface/Job evidence, environment mgmt

Management designs against COSO's 2013 Internal Control — Integrated Framework (ICIF-2013 — verified operative on coso.org at the report date, now surrounded by supplemental guidance: Achieving Effective Internal Control over Generative AI (2026), the second-edition Fraud Risk Management Guide, and Managing Cyber Risk in a Digital Age; five components: control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, monitoring); IT audit maps COBIT objectives onto those components — COBIT 2019 is the current edition (ISACA's credential catalogue still sells "COBIT 19 Foundation" and "COBIT 2019 Design & Implementation"). The external auditor works top-down and risk-based: identify significant accounts, map key controls, test entity-level then ITGC then application controls, and escalate deficiencies by likelihood times magnitude.

What the auditor is actually reading in 2026

The PCAOB's standards list (read via an Internet Archive capture of pcaobus.org dated 31 October 2026) shows the current regime for audits of fiscal years beginning on or after 15 December 2025: AS 1000, "General Responsibilities of the Auditor in Conducting an Audit", is the consolidated general standard, and AS 2201, "An Audit of Internal Control Over Financial Reporting That Is Integrated with An Audit of Financial Statements", remains the operative integrated-ICFR standard — the top-down, risk-based approach above is its method. The same page notes further amendments to auditing standards effective 15 December 2026, i.e. landing mid-compliance- season for calendar-year issuers. For §302 purposes, the SEC adopted the certifications rules as Item 307/308 of Regulation S-K; a cybersecurity incident that is material to investors now has its own 8-K item.

The ITGC-to-SCA translation Notice what the four ITGC domains actually are: change control, access control, secure development, and operational monitoring. That is the same control vocabulary the CRA's Annex I uses for products. The overlap section (§04) exploits exactly this.

03CRA: the clock that is already running

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 — signed at Strasbourg on 23 October 2024, published in the OJ on 20 November 2024, in force 10 December 2024 — applies to any software or hardware product "with digital elements" whose intended purpose or reasonably foreseeable use includes a connection to a device or network, when made available on the Union market in a commercial activity. "Manufacturer" means whoever places it under their own name or trademark, "whether for payment, monetisation or free of charge". Services are not excluded by an Article but by scope mechanics: recital 12 confirms cloud service models — SaaS, PaaS, IaaS — stay under Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2), while a product's own "remote data processing solutions" (Art 3(2)) travel with the product into the CRA. Embedded products, downloadable software and their separately-placed components are in.

What manufacturers must do (Art 13 + Annex I)

Annex I has two parts — Part I, product properties (13 lettered requirements, from "without known exploitable vulnerabilities" to secure-by-default, data protection, attack-surface minimisation and monitoring; verbatim in Appendix A) and Part II, the vulnerability-handling process (8 requirements, SBOM included). Article 13 wraps them: a documented cybersecurity risk assessment across the lifecycle (13(2)–(3)); due diligence so integrated third-party and open source components do not compromise the product (13(5)); reporting vulnerabilities upstream when they come from a component supplier (13(6)); a defined support period — at least five years, or the expected use time if shorter (13(8) third subparagraph) — with its end date, month and year, clearly specified at purchase and an end-of-support notification displayed where technically feasible (13(19)); technical documentation and the EU declaration of conformity kept for authorities for at least 10 years after placing on the market or the support period, whichever is longer (13(13)); CE marking per Annex V before placing on the market.

The reporting clock (Art 14, live since 11 September 2026)

Step Deadline Notes
Early warning 24 h after awareness Actively exploited vulnerability or severe incident
Main notification 72 h Fuller detail to the CSIRT of the main-establishment Member State, via ENISA's SRP
Final report (vulnerability) 14 days after corrective measure available --
Final report (incident) 1 month after the 72 h notification --

Three traps. First, scope: reporting applies to all in-scope products made available on the EU market, including those placed before 11 December 2027 (Art 69(3)) — legacy shipped software is inside the clock. Second, the exemption that isn't: micro and small enterprises cannot be fined for missing the 24-hour deadline, but they still must report. Third, reporting runs through one place only: the CRA Single Reporting Platform (portal.cra-srp.enisa.europa.eu), operational since 11 September 2026, upgraded to version 1.4 on 17 November 2026. Voluntary reports (any person, near-misses included) use the same platform.

The ecosystem around the clock is already exercising: on 21 October 2026 the European Commission, ENISA, CERT-EU, Europol EC3 and the EU CSIRTs network issued a joint statement advising immediate action on critical PHP vulnerabilities — the exact multi-authority coordination shape the CRA institutionalises. On the intelligence side, ENISA's EU Vulnerability Database (EUVD, listed in ENISA's tools catalogue since 13 May 2025) aggregates EU CSIRT and vendor advisories, the EU KEV catalogue and CISA KEV, and is fed by ENISA's roles as a CVE partner — a CNA since January 2024 and a CVE Root since 20 November 2025.

Product classes and conformity routes (Annex III / IV, Art 32)

The class counts matter more than the examples: Annex III Class I has exactly 19 categories, Class II exactly 4, and Annex IV exactly 3 — all listed verbatim in Appendix A. Two definitions decide your reporting and re-assessment triggers (Art 3, verbatim): an actively exploited vulnerability is one "for which there is reliable evidence that a malicious actor has exploited it in a system without permission of the system owner" (Art 3(42)); a substantial modification is a change that affects Annex I compliance or the assessed intended purpose (Art 3(30)) — only substantially modified legacy products re-enter full CRA obligations. "Severe incident" carries no Art 3 definition; recital 68 scopes it to incidents affecting the manufacturer's own development, production or maintenance processes — the canonical example being an attacker introducing malicious code into the release channel for security updates.

Category boundaries are drawn precisely by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2392 of 28 November 2025. Notified bodies have been notifiable since 11 June 2026 (Member States had to designate notifying authorities by then); ENISA has published technical competence requirements for them, and the Commission's implementation milestone expects a sufficient number notified by 11 December 2026.

Free and open source software: the steward deal

The CRA's FOSS settlement, confirmed on the Commission's own open source page (updated 15 October 2026): FOSS is in scope only when made available on the market — supplied in a commercial activity; unmonetised publication is not commercial, and individual contributors are never responsible. A new legal category, the open source software steward (Art 24), captures legal persons that systematically sustain development of FOSS intended for commercial activities: stewards must run a public cybersecurity policy for secure development and vulnerability handling, cooperate with authorities, and report (Art 24(3) applies selected Art 14 duties from 11 December 2027) — but stewards are exempt from administrative fines (Art 64(10)). Community resources the Commission itself links: the OpenChain CRA checklist, the Open Regulatory Compliance Working Group (orcwg.org), OpenSSF's CRA course, and OSI's standards work.

Standards: the machinery is late

Presumption of conformity (Art 27) requires harmonised standards cited in the OJEU. The standardisation request M/606 (Commission document C(2025)618) commissions 41 standards — 15 horizontal (including vulnerability handling) and 26 vertical, product-specific ones. On 10 November 2026 the request was amended (Commission document C(2026)7861) to extend delivery deadlines for all vertical and some horizontal standards by three months — a public admission that the standards layer will not be finished as the December 2027 application date approaches. Related but distinct: the EN 18031 series is currently cited in the OJEU for the Radio Equipment Directive's cybersecurity requirements (Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/30), not for the CRA; the Commission's FAQ notes it intends the CRA to take over those radio-equipment requirements from 11 December 2027. Products certified under other schemes keep their certificates only until 11 June 2028.

04Where they overlap: one control fabric

For a US-listed company that ships software into the EU (and that is most enterprise software companies), the two regimes draw on the same underlying capabilities. The mapping below is the practical core of this report — each row is one investment that satisfies both regulators:

Control fabric SOX anchor CRA anchor Shared evidence artifact
Change management ITGC "program changes": ticket, approval, test, SoD, deploy Annex I secure development; Art 13 lifecycle risk assessment Merge request + CI pipeline + approval log (immutable)
Access management ITGC "access to programs and data"; quarterly user access reviews Not directly regulated — but every CRA control assumes it IdP joiner/mover/leaver log; access review sign-off
Vulnerability & patch handling ITGC operations; ICFR monitoring (unpatched fin systems = deficiency) Annex I Part II; Art 13(5)–(6); Art 14 reporting Scanner output triaged to SLA; SBOM + VEX; disclosure log

What does NOT transfer SOX cares about assertions about financial statements (completeness, accuracy, existence); the CRA cares about properties of a product (attack surface, update mechanism, support period). You cannot reuse a SOX control conclusion as CRA conformity, and an ICFR opinion says nothing about CE marking. The fabric is shared; the attestations are separate documents with separate owners.

Reality check on "CRA compliance platforms" As of the report date, no commercial product can hand you Annex I conformity, because the harmonised standards that define testable conformity are still in development (M/606, extended 10 Nov 2026). 09Risk radar

Ordered by expected cost of getting it wrong.

Blocking · already live

The 24-hour clock applies to shipped products today

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, Art 14 + Art 69(3) · live since 11 Sep 2026

Reporting duties cover actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents in products already made available on the EU market, before full application. A company with no SRP registration, no triage SLA, and no runbook is accumulating unreported-clock risk on its entire shipped catalogue, at €15M/2.5% exposure.

Fix Register on the SRP now; write the 24h early-warning template; assign a named aware-officer function (the clock starts at organisational awareness, not engineer awareness).
Blocking · personal liability

SOX officers sign what the pipeline produces

15 U.S.C. §7241, 18 U.S.C. §§1350, 1519

§302 certifications rest on controls over the systems that produce the numbers. A change-management process that lives in Slack approvals and a UAR done by screenshot is a deficiency factory — and §802 turns "we deleted the old evidence" from housekeeping into a felony.

Fix Make evidence a byproduct of the delivery pipeline (MR approvals, CI records, IdP logs), retained immutably for the audit horizon.
Important · schedule risk

Harmonised standards will arrive late; notified bodies are scarce

Standardisation request M/606 (C(2025)618), amended C(2026)7861 on 10 Nov 2026

The three-month extension for vertical standards means many Class I products will self-assess against Annex I directly (there is no harmonised standard to presume conformity from), and Class II/critical products will queue for the limited notified-body capacity being stood up through 2026–27. Congestion near 11 December 2027 is a scheduling certainty.

Fix Self-assess against Annex I + the OpenChain checklist now; book notified-body capacity early if Annex IV or Class II applies.
Important · scope drift

"Free of charge" is still a commercial activity

Art 3 definitions; Recital 18; Commission OSS page (15 Oct 2026)

The CRA binds manufacturers that place products on the market "for payment, monetisation or free of charge". A free core with a paid support tier is clearly inside; unmonetised hobby publication is out. Companies misreading "free" as "out of scope" is the most common scoping error in the field.

Worth doing · hygiene

Two registers, one incident: dual-report triggers

Form 8-K Item 1.05 vs ENISA SRP

A severe product incident can simultaneously trigger §409 disclosure — since December 2023 via Form 8-K Item 1.05 (materiality judgment, investor lens, four-business-day default) — and Art 14 reporting (24h, security lens). Run one incident register with two trigger checklists; divergent clocks from two teams produce inconsistent disclosures — the classic enforcement path in both regimes.

11Appendix A: Annex I and the product categories, verbatim

Extracted verbatim from the Official Journal text of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 (OJ L series, 20.11.2024, via the Publications Office), so this report carries the operative words rather than a paraphrase. Part I item (2) applies "on the basis of the cybersecurity risk assessment referred to in Article 13(2) and where applicable".

Annex I, Part I — properties of products with digital elements

Item (1) is the chapeau: products "shall be designed, developed and produced in such a way that they ensure an appropriate level of cybersecurity based on the risks." Item (2) requires that they:

Point Requirement (verbatim)
(a) be made available on the market without known exploitable vulnerabilities;
(b) be made available on the market with a secure by default configuration, unless otherwise agreed between manufacturer and business user in relation to a tailor-made product with digital elements, including the possibility to reset the product to its original state;
(c) ensure that vulnerabilities can be addressed through security updates, including, where applicable, through automatic security updates that are installed within an appropriate timeframe enabled as a default setting, with a clear and easy-to-use opt-out mechanism, through the notification of available updates to users, and the option to temporarily postpone them;
(d) ensure protection from unauthorised access by appropriate control mechanisms, including but not limited to authentication, identity or access management systems, and report on possible unauthorised access;
(e) protect the confidentiality of stored, transmitted or otherwise processed data, personal or other, such as by encrypting relevant data at rest or in transit by state of the art mechanisms, and by using other technical means;
(f) protect the integrity of stored, transmitted or otherwise processed data, personal or other, commands, programs and configuration against any manipulation or modification not authorised by the user, and report on corruptions;
(g) process only data, personal or other, that are adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the intended purpose of the product with digital elements (data minimisation);
(h) protect the availability of essential and basic functions, also after an incident, including through resilience and mitigation measures against denial-of-service attacks;
(i) minimise the negative impact by the products themselves or connected devices on the availability of services provided by other devices or networks;
(j) be designed, developed and produced to limit attack surfaces, including external interfaces;
(k) be designed, developed and produced to reduce the impact of an incident using appropriate exploitation mitigation mechanisms and techniques;
(l) provide security related information by recording and monitoring relevant internal activity, including the access to or modification of data, services or functions, with an opt-out mechanism for the user;
(m) provide the possibility for users to securely and easily remove on a permanent basis all data and settings and, where such data can be transferred to other products or systems, ensure that this is done in a secure manner.

Annex I, Part II — vulnerability handling requirements

Point Requirement (verbatim)
1 identify and document vulnerabilities and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a software bill of materials in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the top-level dependencies of the products;
2 in relation to the risks posed to products with digital elements, address and remediate vulnerabilities without delay, including by providing security updates; where technically feasible, new security updates shall be provided separately from functionality updates;
3 apply effective and regular tests and reviews of the security of the product with digital elements;
4 once a security update has been made available, share and publicly disclose information about fixed vulnerabilities, including a description of the vulnerabilities, information allowing users to identify the product with digital elements affected, the impacts of the vulnerabilities, their severity and clear and accessible information helping users to remediate the vulnerabilities; in duly justified cases … they may delay making public information regarding a fixed vulnerability until after users have been given the possibility to apply the relevant patch;
5 put in place and enforce a policy on coordinated vulnerability disclosure;
6 take measures to facilitate the sharing of information about potential vulnerabilities in their product with digital elements as well as in third-party components contained in that product, including by providing a contact address for the reporting of the vulnerabilities discovered in the product with digital elements;
7 provide for mechanisms to securely distribute updates for products with digital elements to ensure that vulnerabilities are fixed or mitigated in a timely manner and, where applicable for security updates, in an automatic manner;
8 ensure that, where security updates are available to address identified security issues, they are disseminated without delay and … free of charge, accompanied by advisory messages providing users with the relevant information, including on potential action to be taken.
Read point 1 again That is a statutory SBOM duty — "commonly used and machine-readable format" at minimum covering top-level dependencies. SPDX and CycloneDX both qualify; the tools in §§05–06 exist to feed exactly this line.
